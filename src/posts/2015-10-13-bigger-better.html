Imagine you are participating in a tough rope-pulling competition. After surviving the first round you may add a second member to your team. Will this double the power?

Social loafing
It may surprise you, but research shows there is a performance loss if a team grows from one individual to two or three. This is the discrepancy between the potential and actual productivity. And it becomes even worse. Because when adding a fourth, fifth and sixth member there occur additional decrements (Ingham et al., 1974). If one individual performs at 100%, then the well-known research by Max Ringelmann shows that this decreases to an average of 93% for a group of two, 85% for a group of three, 77% for a group of four and down to 49% for a group of eight (Moede, 1927). Consequently there is barely any difference in the pulling power of a group of six, seven or eight persons. These finding are called social loafing or the Ringelmann effect (Latané et al., 1979).

Average individual effort scores according to group size (Ingham et al., 1974).

Motivation vs. coordination
The performance loss is caused by a lack of motivation, complex coordination, or a combination of both. First, members will decrease their effort when they feel less responsible for the output (Rond, 2012). This can be either intentionally or unintentionally. However in both cases it affects the individual action and the motivation of the other team members. Compare it with clapping for an artist. When the group grows it is easier to hide in the crowd. In teams that are too large it is very difficult to relate your effort to the collective performance. This missing feedback causes a decrease in responsibility.

Second, coordination is affected by the necessary number of conversations. Professor Richard Hackman (1983) discovered that within a team of five members it takes only ten conversations for every person to touch base. If the group size grows to 13 the number of necessary conversations for every person increases to 78. Research by Richard Lepsinger & Darleen DeRosa (2010) confirms this by noticing that virtual teams with 13 members or more score the worst.
